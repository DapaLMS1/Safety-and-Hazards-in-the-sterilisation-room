Safety & Hazards

Identifying and controlling risks in the sterilisation environment.

The Legal Foundation: WHS in Australia

Every task in a Reprocessing unit or sterilisation department is governed by the Work Health and Safety (WHS) Act.

As a technician, you have a legal "Duty of Care" to ensure your actions do not harm yourself, your colleagues, or the patients. In our industry, workplace safety practices must also align with AS/NZS 4187, which is the national standard for reprocessing reusable medical devices.

Clinical Reasoning

The Standard dictates what must happen to the instruments (introducing the thermal hazard to achieve sterility). The WHS Act dictates how we protect ourselves from that hazard while doing the job.

They work together through Risk Controls: using engineering controls like door interlocks and PPE like thermal gloves so we can safely achieve the required clinical temperatures.

Hazard Mitigation in the Decontamination Area

To ensure staff safety and compliance with AS 5369:2023, you must apply the following controls when handling contaminated Reusable Medical Devices (RMDs):

  • Minimising Aerosolisation: The cleaning process can generate aerosols (a fine mist) that may contain microorganisms. Control: Always keep the lid closed during the operation of an ultrasonic cleaner. When manual scrubbing is required, perform the action under the surface of the water to prevent splashing. PPE: Appropriate fluid-resistant PPE, including face protection (mask and eye protection/face shield), is mandatory to prevent inhalation or mucosal exposure.
  • Prevention of Sharps Injuries: "Hidden sharps" are a high-risk hazard. You must never rely on sight alone to navigate a sink or bath. Control: Never reach blindly into a sink or ultrasonic bath. If you cannot see the bottom clearly, the water must be drained or the items must be retrieved using long-handled forceps or a magnetic retrieval tool. Point-of-Use Safety: Segregate sharps at the point of use. Sharps should be removed into a dedicated sharps container before the tray is transported to the decontamination area.
  • Chemical Safety and Management: Enzymatic detergents are designed to digest organic proteins; they do not distinguish between the proteins on a dental bur and the proteins in your skin or eyes. Control: Always consult the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) before using any cleaning agent. The SDS will specify the required dilution ratios and the appropriate PPE (e.g., chemical-resistant gloves vs. standard examination gloves). First Aid: Ensure an eye-wash station is clearly marked.

You are about to hand-wash a tray of heavily soiled dental instruments. Why is relying only on your PPE (apron, mask, gloves) considered the weakest way to protect yourself from contaminated aerosols?

Clinical Reasoning

PPE is the last line of defence—it only protects you and is prone to failure (e.g., a poorly fitted mask or a tear in a glove).

By changing your work practice (an administrative control)—such as keeping the instruments completely submerged under the water while brushing—you stop the contaminated aerosols from being created in the first place. This protects everyone in the wash room, not just you.

Phase Two Hazards: Sterilisation (Heat & Pressure)

Safety in the sterilisation area involves managing the extreme conditions required to kill microorganisms. Once cleaned, items move to the clean zone where the hazard shifts primarily to thermal and chemical risks.

  • Thermal Safety: Autoclaves operate at temperatures exceeding 134°C.
    • No Jewellery: Jewellery such as neck chains or facial jewellery must not be worn. On opening after a cycle has completed, the unit emits hot steam and any metallic items on your body will rapidly absorb heat from the unit causing burn injuries.
    • Door Operation: Never attempt to force a door open during an active cycle.
    • Handling Aids: Always use heat-resistant aids (like tray lifters or thermal gloves) when removing loads.
    • Cooling Down: Allow loads to cool completely before handling them to prevent "steam burns" from trapped moisture releasing.
  • Chemical Safety: When using low-temperature sterilants or detergents, you must comply with the Safety Data Sheets (SDS). These documents provide essential instructions on first aid, storage, and spill management protocols.
  • Biological Safety (Cycle Failures): If parameters are not met, the cycle is considered a failed cycle and the load is deemed contaminated with microorganisms. The whole load content must be impounded, stripped, and re-processed from the start to prevent the risk of infection transmission.

Clinical Reasoning

Chemical indicators only demonstrate that steam reached them and caused a chemical reaction. They do not prove that the exact lethality conditions required to kill highly resistant bacterial spores were maintained for the required time.

The digital/physical printout is the definitive legal proof of the physical parameters. If the parameter fails, the load is legally non-sterile, regardless of what the tape looks like.

Universal Hazards: Manual Handling

Sterilisation tasks are repetitive and often involve heavy weights. Regardless of which "side" of the department you are working on, awareness of body mechanics is essential to prevent musculoskeletal injuries.

  • The "Push vs. Pull" Rule: Always push a trolley rather than pulling it.
    • Body Weight: Pushing allows you to utilize your body weight for momentum, reducing asymmetrical strain on the shoulder joints and lower back.
    • Visibility: Pushing ensures you have a clear line of sight, preventing collisions.
  • Ergonomic Workstations: Adjust bench heights to waist/elbow level where possible to avoid awkward postures and slouching when packing hundreds of instruments.
  • Task Rotation: Move between different roles (e.g., changing between chair side assisting and reprocessing area duties) to rest specific muscle groups and reduce the risk of repetitive strain injuries.

You have been inspecting and pouching fine, intricate dental instruments (like endodontic files and sharp scalers) for three straight hours. You feel a dull ache in your forearms and your eyes are strained. Why is "pushing through the pain" to finish the batch considered a clinical risk as well as a WHS risk?

Clinical Reasoning

The WHS risk is clear: ignoring the pain leads to acute musculoskeletal injuries, like repetitive strain injury (RSI).

The clinical risk is that physical fatigue directly impairs your fine motor skills and concentration. If your hands are tired and your eyes are strained, you are highly likely to miss residual cement or blood on a fine scaler, or accidentally puncture the paper side of the sterile pouch with a sharp tip. This directly compromises the safety of the next dental patient.
